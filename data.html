1  Preliminaries

1.1 Sample

Chapter 2 uses the annual teaching sample of Bali et al. (2016), covering U.S. common stocks at each year-end from 1988 through 2012. The empirical replications use longer monthly samples.

The examples read local Parquet files through era_pl.load_parquet(). The DATA_DIR setting points to the data root, and each table lives at DATA_DIR/schema/table.parquet. You can create these files from WRDS with the setup script in Section 1.1.2.

1.1.1 WRDS tables

The table lists the inputs used in this build. The source update date comes from the metadata embedded in each Parquet file, read with db2pq. It is not the sample end date or the date the file was downloaded.

ff.factors_daily Fama–French Daily market excess returns for the crash checks in Section 7.3 and for estimating daily betas. 2026-06-16

The replications use legacy CRSP (SIZ), the format available when BEM wrote the book. The local legacy exports are newer than their original snapshot. The newer CIZ files use different return conventions, including the timing of dividend reinvestment. See CRSP’s comparison of the formats.

1.1.2 Create the Parquet repository

Copy .env.example to .env in the book checkout and set DATA_DIR to your Parquet root and WRDS_ID to your WRDS username. After uv sync, run the setup script from that checkout. If you already have the source files there, you can skip this step. If your WRDS account lacks access to the legacy CRSP tables, use licensed SIZ extracts at the corresponding paths.

uv run scripts/download_wrds_tables.py

The following Python example shows how the script uses db2pq.wrds_update_pq() to export the WRDS tables to DATA_DIR.

from db2pq import wrds_update_pq
from dotenv import load_dotenv

load_dotenv()

tables = {
    "crsp": ["msf", "dsf", "msenames", "msedelist", "msi", "ccmxpf_lnkused"],
    "comp": ["funda"],
    "ff": ["factors_monthly", "factors_daily"],
}

for schema, names in tables.items():
    for name in names:
        wrds_update_pq(name, schema)

1.1.3 Other inputs

The annual methods chapters construct December betas, size, and book-to-market from the local source data when needed. See Chapters 8, 9, and 10 for how each measure is constructed. The monthly Size and Value analyses read the 12-month daily beta from eap.betas, which the Beta chapter can export during the full render. See the render instructions.

The size builder and the CRSP and Value chapters use the BLS all-items CPI for urban consumers, not seasonally adjusted, from FRED, to express market capitalizations in December 2012 dollars. This file does not come from WRDS.

Chapter 13 uses the December 2013 Pástor–Stambaugh liquidity release bundled with eap_pl.

For comparisons across Fama–French vintages, see eap_pl.get_ff_factors(). The loader checks the archive’s checksum and keeps a local copy for later renders. Chapter 14 and Chapter 15 use the June 2014 archive. The four-factor volatility model also uses its daily momentum factor.

1.2 Winsorization and Truncation

Winsorization caps values outside chosen percentile cutoffs at the corresponding cutoff. Truncation sets those values to missing, so analyses that require the variable exclude those observations. In Section 1.2, Bali et al. (2016) describe both methods as ways to limit the influence of extreme values. They suggest capping observations that still convey information about genuinely high or low values, and consider truncation when the extremes appear to come from a different process. They urge caution when the conclusions depend on which treatment is used.

BEM compute the cutoffs separately for each year or month because they analyze each period’s cross-section before summarizing the results over time. Their 0.5% rule uses the 0.5th and 99.5th percentiles. In Chapter 3, both variables are winsorized for Pearson correlations, and Spearman correlations use ranks of the original values. In Chapter 6, the regressors are winsorized and the excess-return outcome is used as observed.

We compute the winsorization cutoffs by linear interpolation with .era.winsorize(prob=0.005, quantile_method="linear"). The eap_pl.winsorized_corr() helper applies this rule to each variable after keeping pairs with two finite values. In Chapter 8, .over("month") computes cutoffs separately for each month’s beta estimates. We then fit the Fama–MacBeth regressions with statspai.fama_macbeth().

For examples using .era.winsorize() and .era.truncate() and a comparison with robust regression, see Chapter 24 of Empirical Research in Accounting.

1.3 Newey and West (1987) Adjustment

In Section 1.3, Bali et al. (2016) explain why inference on a time-series mean can be sensitive to changing variance and correlation across periods. Regressing a series on a constant gives its sample mean as the intercept. The adjustment of Newey and West (1987) estimates the standard error using covariances across periods, with less weight on more distant lags. Depending on the pattern of autocorrelation, the adjustment can increase or decrease the standard error. In Chapter 5, BEM use the adjustment for average portfolio returns and spreads, and in time-series factor regressions for portfolio alphas.

For Fama–MacBeth regressions, BEM apply the adjustment to the sequence of estimated coefficients, with one estimate per cross-section. BEM motivate six lags with a rule of thumb for a series of about 600 monthly observations and also use six lags in their annual example, which has only 24 coefficient estimates. Gow et al. (2010) call this procedure FM-NW and find that its standard errors can remain too small when the underlying panel has both cross-sectional and time-series dependence. A short coefficient series provides limited information about persistence in the underlying data (Gow et al. 2010, 488).

The annual, beta, size, and book-to-market regressions replicated here use realized excess returns over nonoverlapping holding periods. This resembles the return-based application in Gow et al. (2010, sec. V), where shared shocks across stocks are the main concern. For slope inference, the relevant serial dependence involves the joint behavior of regressors and residuals. Size is highly persistent in our replications. Across specifications, the pooled lag-one correlations between residuals for the same stock range from about \(-0.05\) to \(-0.01\).

For every slope in the monthly replications, the ordinary and six-lag tests agree at the 5% level. Section 6.2 shows how the adjustment changes inference on size in the annual example.

For more on Newey–West, Fama–MacBeth, and clustering, see Section 5.6 of Empirical Research in Accounting in the StatsPAI edition or the Polars edition.
